Feature Scoring

Feature Scoring

A probabilistic, evidence-accumulating approach to species identification

Why a new approach?

Arhopala is one of the most species-rich lycaenid genera in the world, with over 200 species across South and Southeast Asia. Many look strikingly similar in the field — similar size, similar iridescent blue upperside, and subtle differences in the underside spot pattern that require careful, feature-by-feature comparison.

The traditional approach relies on a dichotomous identification key. This works well in ideal conditions, but specimens rarely present themselves ideally: upperside markings may be obscured by a closed wing, a marginal detail worn or damaged, or the observer simply uncertain about a specific character. A single wrong turn in a dichotomous key sends the user entirely off-track.

Feature Scoring was developed to address these limitations — accumulating evidence from whatever is visible, tolerating uncertainty, and narrowing toward the correct species even when some characters cannot be assessed.

How the C&P Key works

The Corbet & Pendlebury key (1992, 4th edition) is a dichotomous key — a sequence of binary choices that branches toward a species. At each step the user chooses between two contrasting character states; the chosen branch determines the next question, and so on until a species is named.

C&P Key — sequential path
Q1 HW tornal lobe present?
  → Yes — proceed to Q2
  → No — proceed to Q14
Q2 Underside pale slatey purple?
  → Yes — proceed to Q3
  → No — proceed to Q8
Q8 HW postdiscal band position…
  → Close to submarginal lunules
    → A. horsfieldi
One wrong answer routes the user to a wrong branch with no path back.

The key's branching structure encodes decades of morphological expertise. When a specimen is complete, fresh, and the observer is confident in each character, the key delivers a precise identification path quickly. Its limitation is structural: a misidentified character, a worn detail, or honest uncertainty propagates irrecoverably through every subsequent step.

How Feature Scoring works

Feature Scoring treats identification as a scoring problem rather than a routing problem. Every species carries a feature matrix — a set of (question, expected answer) pairs drawn from the identification key and supplementary morphological data. As you answer questions, each species accumulates a score based on how well its known features match your answers.

Scoring rule — applied per answer, per species:
A confirmed match earns the species two points; a mismatch costs it one point; the absence of that character in a species' feature matrix is neutral.

+2
Answer matches the species' known feature
−1
Answer contradicts the species' known feature
0
Species has no entry for this character

Candidates are ranked at all times by their score-to-maximum ratio — how much of the available evidence each species has confirmed relative to how much has been presented. This ratio is used rather than raw score so that species with fewer features in their matrix are not systematically disadvantaged.

Adaptive question ordering

Rather than presenting questions in a fixed sequence, Feature Scoring dynamically ranks the next question to ask. The priority criterion is coverage — how many of the current top-ranked candidates have this question as a feature — combined with diversity — how many different answers those candidates give for it. A question that distinguishes many still-plausible candidates ranks earlier than one that applies to only a few.

This ordering updates after every answer. As the pool of plausible candidates narrows, the question order shifts to reflect what is most diagnostic among those that remain.

Tolerating uncertainty

Any question can be skipped by selecting "Cannot determine." This carries no penalty — it means that question contributes nothing for or against any species' score, and identification continues from the next most informative question. A worn upperside, an obscured forewing space, or genuine observer uncertainty is handled gracefully rather than halting the process.

The underside-only path

A practical consequence of the skip mechanism is that Feature Scoring can operate on underside features alone. Upperside markings and certain forewing space characters are marked "Cannot determine," and the system routes through questions whose answers remain assessable from the underside. For many species pairs, the underside carries sufficient discriminating information to reach a confident identification without ever assessing the upperside.

Each species page shows both the full canonical identification path (upperside included) and the underside-only path, so you can compare what the full and partial-evidence routes look like.

Advantages and limitations

Feature Scoring — strengths
  • Works with partial specimens — any unanswerable question is skipped without consequence
  • All species ranked simultaneously; you always see where every candidate stands
  • A wrong answer is recoverable — it penalises the wrong candidate rather than misdirecting the whole search
  • Underside-only path provides confident identification without upperside assessment
  • Question order adapts as the candidate pool narrows
  • Accessible to observers not yet fluent in the key's couplet logic
Feature Scoring — limitations
  • Quality depends on completeness and accuracy of each species' feature matrix
  • Species with very few distinguishing features may remain poorly separated until late in the sequence
  • Does not surface the underlying morphological reasoning the way a key couplet does
  • Closely related species may score similarly throughout if they share most features
  • Recently described or poorly documented species may be underrepresented in the feature matrix
C&P Key — strengths
  • Encodes explicit taxonomic reasoning — each couplet reflects a morphologically meaningful character
  • Short, decisive paths for species with unique early-branching characters
  • Transparent: the user follows an auditable chain of decisions
  • Works well for complete, fresh specimens with all characters visible
C&P Key — limitations
  • One incorrect answer routes to a wrong branch with no recovery mechanism
  • Cannot proceed past a question the observer is unable to answer
  • Order of questions is fixed, regardless of which characters are clearest on the specimen
  • Provides no ranked alternatives if the user suspects the primary identification

How they complement each other

The two methods are most powerful used together. Feature Scoring is structured around the same morphological characters and species groupings as the C&P key — the feature matrix was assembled from the key's couplet text and supplementary literature, so both are built on the same biological foundation.

Step 1
Feature Scoring
Answer whatever is assessable on the specimen. The ranking narrows the field to a small set of strong candidates, often to a single top match, without requiring every character to be visible.
Step 2
C&P Confirmation
Take the top candidate from Feature Scoring into the key. The key's couplet chain confirms the identification, exposes the explicit morphological reasoning, and catches any scoring artefact.
Step 3
Check the margin
A wide margin between #1 and #2 means high confidence. A narrow margin signals the two candidates share most features — inspect the specimen's distinguishing characters more carefully.

The key's branching structure also underpins Feature Scoring's question sequencing: the identification tree defines which species encounter which questions, determining each question's coverage across the candidate pool. Changes to the key propagate through the Feature Scoring order automatically — the two systems share a single data source.

In practice, Feature Scoring handles the common case — a specimen with some characters visible, some obscured — while the C&P key provides authoritative confirmation and an independent verification route. Neither replaces the other; both are weaker used in isolation than used in sequence.

Scope: This implementation covers Arhopala species recorded in Peninsular Malaysia and Singapore — the geographic scope of Corbet & Pendlebury (1992). Species from adjacent regions not treated in that key are outside the current feature matrix.

Source

Feature matrix derived from: Corbet A.S. & Pendlebury H.M. (1992). The Butterflies of the Malay Peninsula, 4th ed. (revised by J.N. Eliot). Malayan Nature Society, Kuala Lumpur. Supplemented by examination of type specimens and published plate material.
